# Your Accessibility Widget Didn't Save You — 265 Businesses Sued With One Installed

You paid for safety. You didn't get it.

That's not an opinion. That's a number: **265 businesses in Q1 2026 were sued for ADA website violations despite already having accessibility overlay widgets installed.**

Let me say that again, slower: Two hundred and sixty-five business owners — restaurant owners, dentists, HVAC companies, retail shops — bought an accessibility widget, pasted the code into their website, believed they were compliant, and **got sued anyway.**

They did everything right. They saw the problem. They took action. They spent the money. And it didn't work.

## The Widget Industry Sold You a Shield That Doesn't Stop Arrows

Accessibility overlays — accessiBe, UserWay, AudioEye, and their competitors — promise to make your website WCAG-compliant with a single line of code. "AI-powered accessibility," they say. "Install in two minutes." "Compliance guaranteed."

The FTC disagrees.

In January 2025, the Federal Trade Commission **fined accessiBe $1 million** for making deceptive claims that its AI widget could make websites compliant with WCAG guidelines. The FTC found that accessiBe had even **fabricated fake third-party reviews** to bolster their credibility.

One million dollars. That's the federal government saying, explicitly and publicly: **these claims are lies.**

But the fine didn't stop the lawsuits against widget users. If anything, it accelerated them. Plaintiff attorneys now specifically target sites with accessibility overlays, because:

1. **The overlay proves you knew about the requirement.** You can't claim ignorance when you already installed a "compliance solution."
2. **The overlay doesn't fix structural problems.** Alt text, keyboard navigation, form labels, heading hierarchy, color contrast — these require code-level changes, not a JavaScript band-aid.
3. **Judges aren't impressed by widgets.** The Department of Justice has specifically stated that overlays do not satisfy ADA requirements for web accessibility.

## What an Overlay Actually Does (And What It Doesn't)

Here's what an accessibility widget can do:

- Add a toolbar that lets users change font size, contrast, or text spacing
- Provide text-to-speech functionality for page content
- Offer a "read page" mode for people using assistive technology

Here's what it **cannot** do:

- ✗ Add alt text to images that don't have it
- ✗ Fix keyboard traps that prevent navigation without a mouse
- ✗ Label form fields so screen readers can identify them
- ✗ Repair broken heading hierarchies
- ✗ Fix color contrast failures
- ✗ Add skip-navigation links
- ✗ Remove viewport tags that block pinch-zoom
- ✗ Replace missing privacy policies, terms of service, or accessibility statements

In other words: **the overlay makes your site look accessible. It doesn't make your site accessible.**

Think of it this way. Imagine your restaurant's front entrance has a step that wheelchair users can't get over. An accessibility overlay is the equivalent of putting a sign next to the step that says "Wheelchair users: press this button for assistance." The step is still there. The barrier hasn't moved. You just added a sign.

A real fix would be: build a ramp.

## The Real Fix: Code-Level Remediation

Here's the good news: the five fixes that solve 70% of real-world accessibility problems take less than a day to implement.

1. **Alt text on every image.** Every photo of your product, your team, your restaurant — add descriptive alt text. Screen readers will tell visually impaired users what's on the page.
2. **Labeled form fields.** Your contact form should have proper `<label>` elements associated with each input. "Name," "Email," "Message" — not mystery boxes.
3. **Keyboard navigation.** Every interactive element on your site should be reachable and usable with Tab, Enter, and Space. No mouse required.
4. **Heading hierarchy.** One `<h1>` per page, followed by `<h2>`s and `<h3>`s in logical order. Screen readers use headings like a table of contents.
5. **Color contrast.** Text must have sufficient contrast against its background. WCAG 2.1 AA requires a ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text.

These aren't expensive. They're not complicated. They just require someone who knows what they're doing to actually *do the work* — not paste a widget and call it done.
