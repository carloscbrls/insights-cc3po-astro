Reuters reports that hackers breached the U.S. Air Force's flagship cloud platform, Cloud One, after exploiting a software vulnerability. That account comes from three people familiar with the matter. The Air Force has not confirmed it.

In a statement to Reuters, the Air Force said it "does not comment on the security posture, vulnerabilities or operational status of specific networks and systems. We continuously take prudent measures to protect Department (of the Air Force) information, systems and mission capabilities." As of November 27, 2026, that remains a refusal to discuss the report — not a confirmation, and not a denial. Asked later by Fox News to confirm the story, a Department of War official also declined to comment on the status of the department's networks and pointed back to the Air Force.

What Reuters could not establish matters as much as what its sources said. The news agency could not identify the hackers, the full scope of the intrusion, whether any data was stolen, or the specific vulnerability involved. One person told Reuters the intruders used a software flaw to reach the platform's control plane — the management layer that, in theory, could let someone check the status of aircraft or the movement of spare parts. Reuters was clear that it did not know whether anyone actually did that.

This is not a reason to panic about a five-person shop. Most small businesses will never run a military cloud. They do run a mix of laptops, a website, a payment tool, a bookkeeping app, and a handful of plug-ins that do not update themselves. If the reporting holds, the useful part is ordinary: a software vulnerability is how the intruders are said to have gotten in. The work on your side is the same on a quiet week. Know what you run. Patch the internet-facing and already-exploited flaws first. Keep a backup you have actually restored.

## What Reuters reported

The <a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/us/hackers-breach-us-air-force-flagship-cloud-one-computing-platform-2026-11-27/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Reuters exclusive</a>, by Mike Stone and Raphael Satter, was also carried in full by <a href="https://www.militarytimes.com/news/your-military/2026/11/27/hackers-breach-us-air-force-flagship-cloud-one-computing-platform/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Military Times</a> and <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/27/hackers-breach-us-air-force-flagship-cloud-one-computing-platform.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">CNBC</a>, and it was posted on X from <a href="https://x.com/Reuters/status/2126279085215391931" target="_blank" rel="noopener">@Reuters</a>. Three people familiar with the matter told Reuters that Cloud One had been breached. Two of them said the intrusion was discovered within the past month or so, during work to upgrade the system, and that the discovery led to disruption across the platform.

Cloud One began in 2017 as a common cloud for Air Force IT, using services from Microsoft, Amazon, Oracle, and Alphabet, and hosting applications that handle personnel data, pay, logistics, and maintenance, Reuters reported. Among the systems the story names are Cloud-Based Command and Control, which gathers air-defense radar and other feeds from across North America, and the Theater Integrated Combat Munitions System, which tracks bombs and missiles at more than 200 Air Force locations.

Investigators have called in U.S. Cyber Command to assess the damage, one of the people told Reuters. Cyber Command did not respond to Reuters' requests for comment.

After the discovery, two of the original sources and a fourth person said migrations of back-office applications onto Cloud One were paused. Updates to applications already running on the platform were blocked, they said, leaving personnel unable to apply fixes at the same time a new batch of serious software vulnerabilities was being disclosed across the industry. A fifth person said some users had their administrator rights revoked. All five spoke on condition of anonymity.

That last point is easy to misread. The blocked updates, if the reporting is accurate, came after the intrusion was found. They are described as part of the response, not as proof that the original entry was a patch that someone forgot to install. Reuters said one source attributed the break-in to a software vulnerability. It did not identify that vulnerability, and it did not say a fix had already been available.

Until the Air Force or another official body confirms, denies, or adds detail, that is the record: a serious report, still unconfirmed, with large gaps.

## Five things to do this week

The checklist below is not a claim that your shop is next, and it does not depend on the Cloud One report being proven. It is the unglamorous work CISA and NIST already recommend: inventory, automatic updates, a short priority list, attention to vendors, and a backup you have tested.

### 1. Write down what you actually run

You cannot patch a system you do not know you have. CISA's <a href="https://www.cisa.gov/cybersecurity-performance-goals-2-0-cpg-2-0" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Cybersecurity Performance Goals</a> ask organizations to keep a regularly updated inventory of their assets — hardware, software, systems, and the data on them — and to refresh the list more often for anything the business cannot operate without.

NIST's <a href="https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/40/r4/final" target="_blank" rel="noopener">guide to enterprise patch management</a> makes the same point in plainer language. The first job in a patching program is knowing when a new flaw affects you, and that "involves knowing what assets your organization uses and which software and software versions those assets run." NIST recommends a close-to-comprehensive inventory that is kept current, including laptops, phones, cloud apps, and the other systems that tend to fall off a mental list.

For a small shop, a spreadsheet is enough to start. List every laptop and phone, the office router, the website and its plug-ins, the payment tool, the bookkeeping app, email, file storage, and any contractor or vendor login that holds customer or payment data. Note who administers each one, and whether it faces the internet.

### 2. Turn on automatic updates where you can

NIST is blunt about volume: "There is no way that an organization can keep up with patching without automation because of the sheer number of assets, software installations, vulnerabilities, and patches." Automation, it adds, is also what you need in an emergency, "like patching a severe vulnerability that attackers are actively exploiting."

On a small-business scale, that usually means switching on automatic updates for operating systems, browsers, phones, and the plug-ins on your site, then checking that they are actually applying. For tools that will not update themselves — a point-of-sale box, an older appliance, a line-of-business app with a manual installer — put a recurring reminder on the calendar and assign a name to the job. A patch that sits in an inbox is not a patch.

### 3. Fix internet-facing systems and Known Exploited Vulnerabilities first

CISA's performance goals note that adversaries "frequently target unpatched and misconfigured systems, particularly those exposed to the internet." When a new flaw is announced, start with anything customers, vendors, or the public can reach: the website, email, a VPN, a remote-access tool, a customer portal.

Then check CISA's <a href="https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities-catalog" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog</a>. CISA calls it "the authoritative source of vulnerabilities that have been exploited in the wild," and it <a href="https://www.cisa.gov/known-exploited-vulnerabilities" target="_blank" rel="noopener">strongly recommends</a> that all organizations — not only federal agencies — review it, monitor it, and prioritize the flaws on the list. Federal civilian agencies have binding remediation timelines under <a href="https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/directives/bod-26-04-prioritizing-security-updates-based-risk" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Binding Operational Directive 26-04</a>, which treats internet-facing assets and catalogued vulnerabilities as the highest priority. That directive does not bind a private shop. The priority order is still a good model: internet-facing first, then anything CISA already knows is being used in attacks.

If a system cannot be patched, CISA's guidance is to apply compensating controls — for example, taking it off the public internet — and to record that decision. Leaving an outdated, internet-facing tool in place with no mitigating step is the risk to avoid.

### 4. Watch your vendors, and read the patch notices

A store that runs on a payments platform, a booking tool, or a managed IT provider is only as current as those vendors are. CISA's performance goals include a simple supply-chain practice: contracts and service agreements should require vendors to notify you of security incidents and vulnerabilities within a time frame you have actually thought about.

Even without a formal clause, you can do the practical version this week. For every tool on your inventory that holds customer or payment data, subscribe to the vendor's security or status emails, and turn on any in-product security notifications. When a message says a vulnerability was fixed or that you need to update, treat it as a task, not as marketing. If a vendor will not say how it notifies customers about flaws, that is useful information too.

### 5. Keep backups, and test a restore

Patching and backups belong together. A bad update, or a lock-up after a compromise, is when you find out whether you can get the shop back.

CISA's advice for small and medium businesses is the <a href="https://www.cisa.gov/audiences/small-and-medium-businesses/secure-your-business/back-up-business-data" target="_blank" rel="noopener">3-2-1 rule</a>: three copies of important files, on two different kinds of storage, with one copy off-site. Use a backup that runs on a schedule, keep a copy offline, and encrypt it if you can. CISA also says to test that you can restore data fully and partially, and that you can roll back at least seven days if needed.

The same idea appears in CISA's performance goals: store backups off-site and offline, test backup and recovery at least once a year, and check the integrity of a backup before you restore it. A backup that has never been restored is a hope, not a plan. Pick one critical system this week — bookkeeping, email, or the website — and do a test restore to a safe place.

## The point

The Cloud One story, as of today, is a Reuters report based on anonymous sources. Officials have not confirmed it, and they have not denied it. Reuters could not say who was responsible, what was taken, or which vulnerability was used.

That is not a thin reason to skip the checklist. You still need a list of what you run, automatic updates where they exist, a habit of fixing internet-facing and already-exploited flaws first, a way to hear from your vendors, and a backup you have actually opened. Those steps are useful on a quiet week. They are the same steps that matter if a report like this one is later confirmed.
