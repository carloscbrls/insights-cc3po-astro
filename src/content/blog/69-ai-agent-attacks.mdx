The time a small business used to have — the slow afternoon between a first suspicious login and a real break-in — is the thing that just got shorter. Ordinary doors, a reused password, an unpatched plugin, a leftover vendor login, a marketing script nobody has looked at since it was installed, can now be tried at a pace no tired human attacker could keep up. The defense is not a new kind of magic. It is five unglamorous habits, done this week.

## What changed this week

On November 13, the FBI released <a href="https://www.ic3.gov/CSA/2026/261113.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">FLASH-20261113-001</a>, a public advisory on ARTEX, an open-source, AI-driven penetration-testing framework. Since at least August 2026, criminal actors have used it to automate the grind of a break-in: scouting, exploiting, moving sideways, and stealing data. The bureau's wording is careful and worth repeating: "ARTEX significantly reduces the time required to chain attack techniques, with some intrusions progressing from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours." The FLASH also describes a supply-chain compromise of ConnectWise Cloud Backup that led to attempted sign-ins to more than 2,250 downstream tenants — attempted sign-ins, not a confirmed breach of every one of those tenants. The original repository was taken down in October. Copies and forks are still out there.

<Stat num={"Under 3 hrs"} cap={"FBI: some ARTEX-enabled intrusions went from first targeting to privileged access this fast"} />

Three days later, in its <a href="https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/agentic-threat-activity-crosses-a-significant-threshold" target="_blank" rel="noopener">AI Threat Tracker</a>, Google Threat Intelligence Group, with Mandiant, said it had found an exposed staging server running a framework it calls exploit_agent. The system compares popular web software before and after a patch is published, drafts an exploit, and fires it — automatically. Victim data on that server pointed to French media, entertainment, and education websites. Google did not publish a victim count, and it did not attribute to this tool the 22 zero-day exploits it separately counted, industry-wide, in August 2026. What matters is the shrinking gap between "the vendor shipped a fix" and "someone is using the old version against you."

That gap showed up again on Tuesday. Oracle and OpenJDK patched CVE-2026-114938, a memory-corruption bug in Java's Base64 decoder that researchers dubbed Java4Shell. <a href="https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2026/11/17/critical-java-vulnerability-requires-immediate-patching" target="_blank" rel="noopener">CISA urged organizations to patch immediately</a>, especially on internet-facing systems, and to look for Java bundled inside other software. Researchers at Calif, led by Quang Luong, said they used OpenAI's Codex to find the bug in about an hour and build a first working exploit in two more, with a <a href="https://calif.io/java4shell/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">total token cost under $200</a>. In their tests, default installs of Keycloak, Atlassian Jira, and Jenkins were remotely exploitable without a login. The bug only triggers on certain modern Intel and AMD processors, not on ARM. If you run Java, treat this as a patch-now problem.

Then there was the script, which we covered in <a href="/blog/68-audit-the-agent/">Monday's briefing</a>. On November 16, attackers exploited a flaw in Optimizely's Web Experimentation signup flow, impersonated customer administrators, and injected malicious JavaScript into project code those websites already trusted. Scott Helme and his team at Report URI found <a href="https://blog.report-uri.com/worlds-largest-brands-infected-with-malware-via-optimizely/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">188 infected files belonging to 178 organizations</a>, including Zoom, CNN, the BBC, and HSBC. The payload tried to steal crypto and card details typed into a site's own forms, and to exploit older iPhones and iPads. Cards entered in hosted payment iframes such as Stripe or Adyen were out of reach. Optimizely says the first malicious code was written at 07:51 UTC and that it had <a href="https://pages.optimizely.com/rs/947-RKE-048/images/Security%20Incident%20Report%20-%20Optimizely%20Web%20Experimentation%20-%20Nov%2017%202026.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">removed the injected code from all affected projects by 22:24 UTC</a> the same day. Sites with a strong Content Security Policy were protected.

<Quote>"You wouldn't dream of deploying unreviewed, unmonitored code to your production servers, yet we routinely allow third parties to do exactly that in our visitors' browsers." — <a href="https://x.com/Scott_Helme/status/2122680971539419584" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Scott Helme</a></Quote>

Most small businesses do not use Optimizely. Most do load something like it: a chat widget, a booking calendar, an analytics tag, a review plugin, an advertising pixel.

## Five things to do this week

### 1. Turn on MFA everywhere that holds the keys

The FBI's ARTEX advisory does not, in so many words, tell you to turn on multi-factor authentication. What it does describe is attackers abusing logins, vendor credentials, and cloud app permissions, and moving fast once they have a foothold. <a href="https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/fbi-warns-artex-ai-hacking-tool-is-powering-global-cyberattacks/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Reporting on an ARTEX-linked campaign</a> includes password spraying. The accounts that matter most are email, the domain registrar, the website or CMS admin, the cloud console, the payment dashboard, and any vendor portal that can publish to your site.

Turn on multi-factor authentication on all of those today. Prefer an authenticator app or a passkey over a text message when the service offers it. If an old employee or a former freelancer still has a login, remove it first.

### 2. Patch the internet-facing stuff first, and do it fast

Java4Shell is the week's exhibit A, but the habit matters more than this one bug. When a fix ships, assume an exploit is not far behind. Make a short list of your website platform, plugins, booking or store software, and any Java apps you actually run. Ask your host whether Java is hiding inside something you would not think to update. CISA's advice is the right one: inventory first, then patch internet-facing systems first.

### 3. Audit the third-party scripts and plugins on your site

Open your website. Write down every script and plugin that is not yours: analytics, chat, fonts, booking widgets, review tools, advertising tags. For each one, ask three questions. Do we still use this? Who has the login to change it? If it went bad at 8 a.m., how would we notice?

If your site can use a Content Security Policy, turn one on, even a careful one. Optimizely's own report said a strong CSP stopped the skimmer, because the malicious code tried to load from a domain the site had not authorized. You need a list, and you need to delete the things you cannot explain.

### 4. Revoke old vendor access

This is the step most businesses skip, and the one the FBI spends real time on. Review every app, integration, and contractor account that can read your email, touch your cloud, or publish to your website — including tools you no longer use. The bureau told organizations to review Microsoft 365 tenants where a vendor app is installed or was installed in the past, and to revoke unnecessary permissions and rotate credentials if anything looks off.

Translate that into an afternoon: open your website admin, your Google or Microsoft account, your registrar, and your payment provider. Remove unused apps. Downgrade anyone who does not need full admin. Change passwords on shared logins, then stop sharing them. If a marketing tool or a "just for the launch" account is still connected, it still counts as a door.

### 5. Watch for the quiet changes

You do not need a security operations center to notice the obvious. Turn on login alerts for admin accounts. Watch for new administrators, new plugins, unexpected script changes, and a burst of failed sign-ins. The FBI recommends monitoring new credentials, new privileges, and unusual app-only activity, and putting rate limits on actions that hurt if they run at machine speed — mass emails, mass record lookups, large exports.

Set a calendar reminder to look once a week. Fifteen minutes. If something changed and nobody on your team did it, that is the whole alarm.

## The point

You will not outrun an AI agent in a fair fight, and you should not try. These attacks still walk through ordinary doors: a password without a second factor, a patch that sat for a week, a vendor login from last year, a script trusted because it was trusted last month.

Close those doors this week. Then keep them closed. That is the whole defense.
