# California Small Businesses Are Getting Sued Over Website Tracking — Here's What to Do

If you run a business in California — a plumbing company, a restaurant, a retail shop, a dental practice — someone might be suing you right now over how your website tracks visitors.

You probably haven't heard of CIPA. But the people filing these lawsuits have. And they're targeting small businesses specifically.

Here's what's happening, why it matters, and what you can do today to protect yourself.

## What Is CIPA (And Why Should You Care)?

The **California Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA)** was originally written in 1962 to protect Californians from wiretapping and eavesdropping. It predates the internet by decades.

But in recent years, plaintiff attorneys have repurposed CIPA to target businesses that use website tracking technologies — things like:

- **Google Analytics** (tracks visitor behavior)
- **Facebook Pixel** (tracks conversions for ads)
- **Chat widgets** (often collect visitor data before consent)
- **Session recording tools** (record mouse movements, clicks, scrolling)
- **Third-party cookies** (track browsing across sites)

Under the argument that these tools "intercept" or "record" electronic communications without the visitor's consent, plaintiffs are filing CIPA lawsuits claiming statutory damages of **up to $5,000 per violation**.

Here's the math that should terrify you: if 100 visitors came to your website and were tracked without explicit consent, that's potentially **$500,000 in damages**. Most businesses settle for $5,000–$25,000 because fighting is more expensive than paying.

## Who's Being Targeted?

This is not a big-tech problem. The Sacramento Bee's investigation (May 14, 2026) revealed that the primary targets are **small local businesses**:

- **Plumbers** — local service businesses with simple websites
- **Restaurants** — using reservation systems and online ordering
- **Retail shops** — with e-commerce or inventory tracking
- **Dental and medical offices** — with patient portals and appointment scheduling
- **Auto repair shops** — with service booking forms
- **Salons and spas** — with booking widgets

These businesses share common characteristics:

1. **They have websites with tracking code** (usually Google Analytics or a Facebook Pixel added by their web developer or marketing agency)
2. **They don't have updated privacy policies** that specifically disclose tracking
3. **They don't have a cookie consent banner** or visitor consent mechanism
4. **They're small enough to settle** — they don't have in-house legal teams

Sound familiar? If your California business has a website — and most do — you're in the target zone.

## The Repeat Plaintiff Problem

The Sacramento Bee investigation identified **Jimmy Atteberry** as a serial CIPA plaintiff who has filed lawsuits against **dozens of California businesses**.

Here's how it typically works:

1. **Atteberry visits your website** — or claims to
2. **His attorneys allege tracking violations** — your Google Analytics, chat widget, or cookie usage "intercepted" his electronic communications without consent
3. **A demand letter arrives** — offering to settle for a specific amount, often $5,000–$15,000
4. **Most businesses settle** — because defending a CIPA lawsuit costs $25,000–$50,000 in legal fees

This is the same playbook used in ADA website lawsuits: volume-driven litigation targeting businesses that are least likely to fight back.

Atteberry isn't alone. Multiple repeat plaintiffs and their law firms are systematically scanning California business websites, identifying tracking technologies, and filing claims.

---

## The $5,000 Per Violation Math

CIPA provides for statutory damages of **$5,000 per violation**. And the definition of "violation" is broad:

- **Each visitor tracked** without explicit consent can be a separate violation
- **Each tracking technology** (Analytics + Pixel + chat widget) can compound the claim
- **Each page visit** can be argued as a separate interception

A restaurant with Google Analytics and a Facebook Pixel that tracks 200 visitors without a consent mechanism? That's potentially:

- 200 visitors × 2 tracking technologies = **400 violations**
- 400 × $5,000 = **$2,000,000 in potential damages**

In practice, settlements range from $5,000 to $25,000. But the threat of a massive judgment pressures businesses into settling quickly.

## SB 690: Help That Hasn't Arrived Yet

California **Senate Bill 690** would amend CIPA to exempt tracking done for "commercial business purposes" — meaning routine analytics, advertising, and customer service tools wouldn't trigger CIPA liability.

This would be a game-changer for small businesses. But as of May 2026:

- **SB 690 has not passed** the California legislature
- **It has not been signed** into law by the governor
- **Even if it passes**, it may not retroactively protect businesses already facing lawsuits

You cannot rely on SB 690 to protect you today. The bill is progress, but it's not law yet.

## What to Do Right Now

If you're a California business with a website, here's your protection checklist:

### 1. Audit Your Tracking Technologies

Know exactly what's running on your website:

- Log into Google Analytics, Google Tag Manager, or your CMS
- Check for: Google Analytics, Facebook Pixel, Google Ads conversion tracking, chat widgets (Intercom, Drift, Tawk.to), session recording tools (Hotjar, FullStory), any third-party cookies
- List every tool that collects visitor data

### 2. Update Your Privacy Policy

Your privacy policy must explicitly disclose:

- **What data you collect** (IP addresses, browsing behavior, device info)
- **Which third-party tools** collect data on your site (name them specifically)
- **How data is used** (analytics, advertising, customer service)
- **How visitors can opt out** (provide clear instructions)
- **Your contact information** for privacy-related inquiries

A vague "we may collect data" policy isn't enough. Be specific.

### 3. Implement a Cookie Consent Banner

This is the single most impactful step you can take:

- Use a consent management platform (CMP) like OneTrust, Cookiebot, or Termly
- **Block all non-essential tracking** until the visitor gives consent
- Provide clear "Accept All," "Reject All," and "Customize" options
- Keep a record of consent — date, time, IP address, what they consented to

The key: tracking should **not** start until consent is given. Pre-loaded tracking without consent is exactly what CIPA plaintiffs target.

### 4. Review Your Chat Widgets

Chat tools (Intercom, Drift, Tawk.to, ZenDesk) are a major CIPA target because they often:

- Load before consent is given
- Collect visitor data (pages visited, time on site, device info)
- Transmit data to third-party servers

Configure your chat widget to **load only after consent**. If that's not possible, switch to a privacy-compliant alternative or remove it until you can implement proper consent.

### 5. Add a "Do Not Sell My Personal Information" Link

Under the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), businesses that meet certain thresholds must include a "Do Not Sell My Personal Information" link. Even if you're below the threshold, adding one shows good faith compliance.

### 6. Document Your Compliance Efforts

If you receive a demand letter, your best defense is demonstrating that you took reasonable steps to comply:

- Screenshots of your consent banner
- Copies of your updated privacy policy
- Records of when you implemented these changes
- Audit of all tracking technologies on your site

Courts and opposing counsel take documented compliance efforts seriously.

## What This Means for Non-California Businesses

If you're outside California, you might think this doesn't apply to you. Not quite:

- **If any of your website visitors are in California**, CIPA applies to you. Physical location of the business doesn't matter — location of the plaintiff does.
- **Other states are watching.** Illinois has BIPA (biometric privacy), Colorado has the CPA, Virginia has the VCDPA. Privacy lawsuits are a nationwide trend.
- **Federal privacy legislation is coming.** When it arrives, it will likely include private right of action provisions similar to CIPA.

The compliance steps you take for California today prepare you for nationwide privacy requirements tomorrow.

## The Bottom Line

CIPA lawsuits are the new ADA lawsuits — a volume-driven legal strategy targeting small businesses who don't know they're at risk until a demand letter arrives.

The difference? Most business owners have heard of ADA compliance. Almost nobody has heard of CIPA — and that's exactly what the plaintiffs are counting on.

**$5,000 per violation. Dozens of businesses being sued by repeat plaintiffs. A fix that hasn't become law yet.**

Don't wait for SB 690. Don't wait for a demand letter. Audit your tracking, update your privacy policy, and implement consent — today.
