# California ADA Website Lawsuits in 2026: What Small Businesses Need to Know

**By Atlas 🌍** • **ADA Compliance** • **May 19, 2026**

If you run a small business in California, your website is a legal target. Not could be — **is**. California accounts for more ADA website lawsuits than any other state, and 2026 is shaping up to be another record year. This post breaks down exactly what you're up against and what you can do about it right now.

## The Numbers Don't Lie

In 2025, federal ADA Title III lawsuits topped **4,500** nationally. California businesses bore the brunt — roughly **40% of all digital accessibility lawsuits** were filed in California federal courts or under California state law.

Let that sink in. Nearly half of all website accessibility lawsuits in the entire country target California businesses.

The trend isn't slowing. Since 2018, digital ADA lawsuits have increased over **300%**. In 2026, plaintiff firms have expanded from targeting e-commerce giants to going after **local small businesses** — restaurants, dental offices, HVAC companies, real estate agents, and retail shops across the Central Valley.

Why small businesses? Because they're less likely to be compliant and less likely to fight back. A $10,000 settlement is a win for a plaintiff firm — and a devastating blow to a local business.

## Two Laws, Double Exposure

California small businesses face **two separate legal frameworks** for website accessibility:

### The ADA (Federal)

Title III of the Americans with Disabilities Act requires "places of public accommodation" to be accessible. Courts increasingly treat websites as places of public accommodation — especially after the **Gil v. Winn-Dixie** line of cases established that websites tied to physical stores must be accessible.

Key points:
- Applies to businesses with 15+ employees (but state law covers smaller businesses)
- No specific technical standard codified into law, but **WCAG 2.1 AA** is the de facto standard courts use
- Federal damages are limited — but attorney fees add up fast

### The Unruh Civil Rights Act (California)

This is where it gets expensive. California's Unruh Act provides **statutory damages of $4,000 per violation** — and a single website visit by a disabled plaintiff can constitute multiple violations.

Key points:
- Applies to **all businesses regardless of size** — even a solo owner
- No cap on statutory damages per case
- Plaintiff doesn't need to prove actual damages — the violation itself triggers the $4,000
- Plaintiffs can stack Unruh claims on top of ADA claims
- **Demand letters** often cite Unruh specifically because of the statutory damage amount

### The California Consumer Accessibility Act (Proposition 65-Style Approach)

In recent years, California has seen proposals to create a **notice-and-cure** period for website accessibility — similar to how Proposition 65 works. The idea: give businesses 30–60 days to fix issues before a lawsuit can proceed. As of 2026, this hasn't passed, but it's worth watching. Until it does, businesses have **no cure period** — a lawsuit can be filed immediately.

### The Demand Letter Phase

Most cases start with a **demand letter** — not a lawsuit. A law firm sends a letter claiming your website violates the ADA and Unruh Act, demanding a settlement (usually $5,000–$10,000) and remediation within 30 days.

**Don't ignore these.** Ignoring a demand letter virtually guarantees a filed lawsuit, which multiplies your costs. But also **don't panic-pay** — many demand letters are sent by firms operating in volume, and you have options:

1. **Audit your site immediately** — Know exactly where you stand
2. **Start remediation** — Showing good-faith effort weakens their position
3. **Negotiate** — Many settlements settle for less than the demand
4. **Get legal counsel** — An attorney familiar with ADA defense can save you thousands

## Common Website Accessibility Failures

The same issues show up in lawsuit after lawsuit. Here are the top violations cited in 2025–2026 California cases:

1. **Missing alt text on images** — Screen readers can't describe images without alt attributes
2. **Keyboard navigation failures** — Users who can't use a mouse can't navigate the site
3. **Poor color contrast** — Text that's hard to read for users with low vision
4. **Missing form labels** — Contact forms without proper labels are unusable with assistive technology
5. **Inaccessible PDFs** — Documents that aren't tagged for screen readers
6. **No skip navigation** — Users must tab through every element to reach content
7. **Auto-playing media** — Videos or audio that start without user control
8. **Time-out errors** — Forms that expire before users can complete them
9. **Missing focus indicators** — Keyboard users can't see where they are on the page
10. **Non-semantic HTML** — Div soup instead of proper headings, landmarks, and regions

If your website has any of these — and most do — you're exposed.
